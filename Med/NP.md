## Straight answer for your situation

**For the 103 NP designation, you probably do *not* need to start over.** Your **one year in women’s health** and your **one year in primary care** can likely count toward the California 103 NP transition-to-practice requirement **if** they were:

1. In California.
2. After you were certified/licensed by the California BRN as an NP.
3. Direct patient care as an NP.
4. Within the last 5 years when you apply.
5. Attested by eligible provider(s) who can truthfully verify the hours.

California’s current statute says the 103 pathway requires **3 full-time-equivalent years or 4,600 hours** of transition-to-practice/direct patient-care experience, and an NP with **3 FTE years or 4,600 hours in direct patient care within the last 5 years** may be deemed to have met the requirement. ([Legislative Information][1])

**You do not need one single physician to sign off on all three years.** The BRN process allows **provider(s)** to attest to the hours, and the 103 application instructions state that the providers entered must add up to at least **4,600 hours**. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][2])

So, for you: **the cleanest interpretation is that your women’s health year can count, your current primary care year can count, and you likely need only the remaining eligible hours needed to reach 4,600 hours / 3 FTE years for 103 NP.** You do **not** appear to need three years under the same physician, and you do **not** appear to need to restart a full three-year clock at your current primary care job.

The only practical problem is proof. If no eligible provider from the women’s health practice will attest to that year, you may have trouble using those hours—not because the law says they cannot count, but because the BRN verifies the hours through provider attestation.

# 1. What 103 NP and 104 NP actually mean in California

California AB 890 created two new NP categories that can practice without standardized procedures in defined circumstances; those rules were later amended by SB 1451. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][2])

## 103 NP: “independent practice” only in a group/defined setting

A **103 NP** may practice without standardized procedures, but only in the settings listed in Business and Professions Code § 2837.103, such as clinics, certain health facilities, facilities, medical group practices, home health agencies, and hospice agencies. The BRN describes 103 NP practice as a group-setting model where at least one physician/surgeon practices with the NP. ([Legislative Information][1])

This means **103 NP is not yet the clean solo-from-home independent telehealth practice model** you are ultimately describing. It is a major step, but it is still tied to a qualifying setting/group model.

## 104 NP: the true independent-practice goal

A **104 NP** may practice outside the 103 group settings, within the NP’s education, training, national certification, experience, and scope. The 104 statute also requires consultation/collaboration/referral based on patient condition, plus a referral plan for complex or emergent cases. ([Legislative Information][3])

For your goal—**working from home, under your own license, seeing patients by telehealth, specializing in menopause and weight loss, and billing insurance or private pay**—the cleaner legal target is **104 NP**, not just 103 NP.

# 2. What you need for 103 NP

To apply for 103 NP in California, you generally need:

**Active California NP certification/license status.** The BRN states the applicant must already be certified as a California NP. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][2])

**Current national NP certification recognized by the Board.** The BRN lists national certification as part of 103 eligibility. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][2])

**Transition-to-practice/direct patient care experience:** 3 full-time-equivalent years or 4,600 hours in California, within the last 5 years, after California NP certification, in direct patient care. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][2])

**Attestation by eligible provider(s).** The statute allows attestation by a licensed physician and surgeon, a 103 NP, or a 104 NP. The statute also says the attestor is **not required to specialize in the same category** as the applicant and is **not required to verify clinical competence**, only completion of the required experience. ([Legislative Information][1])

**Important SB 1451 update:** California law now says transition-to-practice clinical experience **shall not be limited to a single NP practice category**, and pre-NP-certification experience cannot be counted. This is a key reason older advice may be conflicting. ([Legislative Information][4])

# 3. Does your women’s health year count?

**Likely yes**, assuming it was after you became a California-certified NP and was direct patient care in California.

Your prior women’s health experience should not be excluded just because it was in a women’s health practice instead of your current primary care practice. Current California law says the clinical experience for transition to practice is **not limited to a single category**, and the attestor does **not** have to be in the same category. ([Legislative Information][4])

That said, there is one important nuance: your **future 103/104 scope must still stay within your education, training, experience, and national certification/population focus**. The BRN says 104 NPs may work independently within the population focus of their national certification, and the 104 statute limits practice to the NP’s education, training, national certification, experience, and competence. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][2])

So:

**If you are an FNP**, your combination of primary care + women’s health is usually the most straightforward for your intended menopause/weight-loss telehealth practice, because FNP scope commonly covers adult women across the lifespan, primary care, prevention, weight management, and menopause-related care when you have training/experience.

**If you are a WHNP**, menopause is very aligned, but broad primary care and weight-loss management should be kept within women’s/gender-related health, your training, and your competence. You would want strong documentation of obesity medicine/primary care training and a referral plan for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, endocrine disorders, eating disorders, severe obesity complications, pregnancy, and complex medication issues.

**If you are AGNP-primary care**, adult women’s menopause and weight management may fit well, but pediatric/family-wide services would not.

The 103-hour-counting issue and the future-practice-scope issue are related, but not identical. **Your women’s health hours can likely count toward 103; your future independent practice must still match your certification and competence.**

# 4. Does the former women’s health physician need to sign off?

**Not necessarily that exact physician, but someone eligible must attest to those hours.**

The best attestor would be the physician or qualifying NP who actually knew your work at the women’s health practice. The statute allows attestation by a licensed physician/surgeon, 103 NP, or 104 NP; BRN FAQ materials also state that attestors are verifying the hours and that they are not being asked to verify competence or clinical expertise. ([Legislative Information][1])

The attestor can also attest to **only part** of the 4,600 hours. Your prior women’s health physician could attest to the women’s health year, and your current primary care provider could attest to the current primary care year and any future hours. The BRN application process is built around adding provider records until the total reaches at least 4,600 hours. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][5])

If you cannot reach the old physician, try the following in this order:

1. The supervising physician from the women’s health practice.
2. Another physician/surgeon at that practice who can truthfully verify your direct patient-care NP hours.
3. A 103 NP or 104 NP at that practice, if there is one and they can truthfully attest.
4. The practice administrator/HR department to help confirm dates, schedules, and route the BRN attestation to the correct clinician.

Staff or HR records may support your request, but the BRN’s process is centered on attestation from eligible providers, not just employment records.

# 5. How much more time do you need?

The legal threshold is **3 full-time-equivalent years or 4,600 hours**, not necessarily exactly three calendar years. ([Legislative Information][1])

Because you already have:

You likely need **approximately one more full-time-equivalent year or whatever number of direct patient-care hours gets you to 4,600**.

A practical way to calculate it:

**Eligible hours already completed**
= women’s health direct patient-care NP hours

* current primary care direct patient-care NP hours

**Remaining hours needed**
= 4,600 − eligible hours already completed

If your first two years were truly full-time, you may be closer than you think. For example, two years at 36–40 hours/week could put you roughly in the 3,700–4,100+ hour range before accounting for PTO, admin time, non-patient-care duties, or part-time weeks. But you need to build the actual hour ledger because BRN and the attestors are focused on **eligible direct patient-care hours**.

**You should not assume you need two more years at the current primary care job.** You likely need only the remaining eligible hours, provided the women’s health year can be attested.

# 6. What about 104 NP later?

This is the part many people mix up.

Your current pre-103 experience helps you get to **103 NP**. But it does **not** satisfy the later **104 NP** practice requirement.

For 104 NP, California law requires that the NP has practiced as an NP in good standing for at least three years, **not inclusive of the transition-to-practice requirement under 103**. ([Legislative Information][3])

The BRN’s 104 NP application instructions are even more direct: the start date for the 4,600 hours toward 104 must be **after obtaining the 103 NP designation**. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][6])

So your likely timeline is:

**Step 1:** Finish enough eligible hours to reach 4,600 / 3 FTE years for 103.
**Step 2:** Apply for and obtain 103 NP.
**Step 3:** Practice as a 103 NP in good standing for another 3 FTE years / 4,600 direct patient-care hours.
**Step 4:** Apply for 104 NP.
**Step 5:** Open the cleaner, independent, home-based telehealth practice model.

If you have a DNP, California law allows the Board to reduce the 104 three-year requirement based on doctoral practice experience, but that is a specific exception and should be verified directly with the BRN. ([Legislative Information][3])

# 7. Can you open your own telehealth practice at 103?

**For the fully independent solo model you described, plan on 104.**

At 103, you can practice without standardized procedures only in the qualifying 103 settings. That may include a clinic or medical group practice, but it still needs to be a qualifying group/setting model with physician involvement in the setting. ([Legislative Information][1])

Before 103, you remain under the standard California NP model using standardized procedures/supervision.

At 104, the law allows practice outside those 103 group settings, but you must still stay within your education, training, national certification, and experience; consult/collaborate/refer when indicated; maintain liability insurance; inform new patients that you are not a physician; and maintain the required referral plan. ([Legislative Information][3])

# 8. Telehealth practice requirements for your future menopause + weight-loss model

For a California-based telehealth NP practice, build the practice around these requirements.

## California telehealth consent

California telehealth law requires the provider to inform the patient about telehealth and obtain verbal or written consent before telehealth services; the consent must be documented. Telehealth does not expand scope of practice, and confidentiality/professional standards still apply. ([Legislative Information][7])

## Patient location and state licensure

For California patients, your California NP authority is the key. If you treat patients physically located in other states, you need to check that state’s NP licensure and telehealth rules. HHS states that the ability to deliver care across state lines varies by state, and CCHP summarizes the general rule that telehealth is typically considered rendered where the patient is located. ([telehealth.hhs.gov][8])

For a simple launch, start with **California patients only** unless you intentionally build a multi-state licensure plan.

## HIPAA-compliant telehealth platform

HHS states that telehealth by covered providers must comply with HIPAA, and covered providers must use vendors that comply with HIPAA and enter into business associate agreements for telehealth technology. ([telehealth.hhs.gov][9])

Do not build the practice around casual FaceTime/Zoom/texting unless your exact setup is HIPAA-compliant and covered by proper BAAs.

## Prescribing and controlled substances

If you prescribe medications, you need to maintain your furnishing authority and comply with California and federal prescribing rules. The BRN states that a furnishing number allows an NP to order/furnish drugs/devices, and Schedule II controlled-substance furnishing requires additional pharmacology content and DEA registration. ([California Board of Registered Nursing][10])

For controlled substances, California’s CURES program applies to prescribers with DEA registration, and CURES consultation is mandatory before first prescribing/furnishing Schedule II–IV controlled substances and at least every six months thereafter when the substance remains part of treatment. ([California DOJ][11])

Federal DEA/HHS telemedicine flexibilities for controlled medications have been extended through **December 31, 2026**, but they remain subject to federal and state law and could change after that. ([DEA][12])

For your niche, this matters if your weight-loss model includes controlled medications or if your menopause model includes any controlled hormone prescribing.

## GLP-1 / compounded weight-loss medication caution

Do not build the business model around mass compounded GLP-1 access without pharmacy/legal review. FDA has determined that the semaglutide injection shortage is resolved and has clarified policies for compounders as national GLP-1 supply stabilized. ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration][13])

A safer business strategy is to build your weight-loss care around comprehensive evaluation, lifestyle/metabolic care, FDA-approved medication pathways when appropriate, insurance prior authorizations, monitoring, labs, contraindication screening, and clear referral criteria—not just “cash-pay compounded GLP-1s.”

# 9. Insurance billing and private-pay setup

Independent practice authority does not automatically make you credentialed with insurance.

For Medicare, CMS says NPs must enroll in Medicare if they provide care to Medicare patients, bill directly under their own NPI, or bill as a supervising NPP for incident-to services; CMS lists I&A, NPPES/NPI, and PECOS/CMS-855 enrollment as part of the process. ([CMS][14])

For Medicare NP services, federal regulation requires the NP to be authorized under state law, nationally certified, and appropriately educated; Medicare also has its own collaboration/documentation concepts even where state law has moved toward independent practice. ([eCFR][15])

For Medi-Cal, DHCS states that NPs may enroll through PAVE as stand-alone providers, and it notes that California law allowed NPs to bill Medi-Cal independently for services. ([DHCS][16])

For private pay, you should also build a Good Faith Estimate workflow. Federal rules require providers/facilities to give good-faith estimates of expected charges to uninsured or self-pay individuals upon request or when scheduling services. ([eCFR][17])

For commercial insurance, expect payer-by-payer credentialing, contracting, CAQH-type profile maintenance, malpractice proof, NPI/tax ID setup, claims clearinghouse setup, EHR documentation standards, telehealth billing rules, and plan-specific rules for obesity medicine, hormone therapy, labs, and prior authorizations.

# 10. Business structure for a future California NP telehealth practice

For a true independent California NP practice, do not assume you can just form a regular LLC and start billing. California has corporate-practice-of-medicine/professional-entity rules. California law recognizes a **nursing corporation** as a professional corporation for rendering nursing services, and the BRN is the governing agency for nursing corporations. ([Legislative Information][18])

California’s 104 NP statute also says corporations and other artificial legal entities have no professional rights, privileges, or powers, which is part of why the entity structure needs to be handled carefully. ([Legislative Information][3])

For your eventual practice, you should have a California healthcare attorney or CPA familiar with NP-owned professional practices help you set up the correct entity, tax structure, payer contracts, malpractice coverage, and policies.

# Bottom line

**For 103 NP:** Your women’s health year should likely count if it was California direct patient-care NP work after your NP certification and within the last 5 years. You do **not** need one physician to sign off on all three years. Multiple eligible providers can attest to different portions of the 4,600 hours. You likely need only the remaining hours—possibly about one more full-time-equivalent year or less/more depending your actual hours—not a fresh three years at your current primary care job.

**For 104 NP:** Your current two years do **not** satisfy the later 104 requirement. After you obtain 103 NP, you need another 3 FTE years / 4,600 hours practicing as a 103 NP in good standing before 104, unless a DNP-related reduction applies.

**For your work-from-home menopause + weight-loss telehealth goal:** Treat 104 NP as the real endpoint. Use the next phase to get 103, build menopause/metabolic-health expertise, document your hours, strengthen prescribing/telehealth compliance, and prepare the professional entity, payer credentialing, HIPAA platform, referral network, and clinical protocols needed for a legitimate independent telehealth practice.
